How to Help Google Discover Your New Website

A practical introduction to Search Console, sitemaps, URL inspection and the difference between discovery and indexing.

By Gagana Chathuranga · Updated September 22, 2026

Launching a website and appearing in Google Search are two separate steps. Google must discover a page, crawl it and decide whether to index it. You can make that process easier to understand and monitor with Google Search Console, but no submission guarantees a search ranking.

1. Publish a useful, accessible page

Before requesting indexing, open the website in a private browser window. Confirm that visitors can access it without signing in, that its main text is visible and that the links work. Give the page a meaningful title and make sure it provides something useful to a real visitor.

A homepage that only lists social buttons can help people find your accounts, but individual guides or project write-ups give visitors more information and give search engines additional pages to understand.

2. Add your site to Google Search Console

Search Console is Google's tool for website owners. It can show whether Google knows about your URLs and report indexing issues. You can verify ownership using a domain-level property or a URL-prefix property. Follow the verification method shown in your account; for a domain property, this commonly involves a DNS TXT record.

Verification proves you control the site. It does not mean Google has indexed every page.

3. Prepare and submit a sitemap

A sitemap is a list of important public page URLs. This site's sitemap is available at /sitemap.xml. When new articles are published, the sitemap should be updated so it includes the new URLs.

In Search Console, open the Sitemaps report and submit the full sitemap address when the interface requires it, for example https://gaganachathuranga.com.lk/sitemap.xml. A successful submission means Google could process the file; it does not promise that every listed page will be indexed.

4. Inspect an individual URL

Use URL Inspection to enter the full address of a page. Search Console may report that the page is already on Google, is discovered but not indexed, or has a technical issue. Read the details before taking action.

If the page is accessible and eligible for indexing, you can request indexing once. Repeated requests do not guarantee faster crawling. For many pages, keeping the sitemap current is more practical than requesting each URL individually.

5. Understand 'Discovered – currently not indexed'

This status means Google knows the URL exists but has not indexed it yet. It does not by itself mean your website is broken. Give Google time, confirm the page loads correctly, and improve the page if it lacks useful original content. Search Console reports can lag behind what is currently happening on your site.

6. Build clear paths to your pages

Link to your articles from your homepage and connect related guides to each other. Put your main website address on the social profiles you already use. These links help people reach the site and give crawlers ways to discover your pages. Avoid buying spammy links or publishing repetitive pages only to target keywords.

7. Measure progress without obsessing over rankings

Check Search Console periodically for indexing coverage, search queries and clicks. Website analytics can show which articles people actually read. If one guide answers a common question, update it when the software or interface changes rather than producing many near-identical articles.

Common questions

How long does indexing take?

There is no fixed deadline. A request can be processed quickly or take longer, and Google may decide not to index a page.

Does a successful sitemap submission mean my site appears in search?

No. A sitemap helps Google discover URLs; indexing is a separate decision.

Should I request indexing every day?

No. Submit a valid request when needed, keep your sitemap updated and focus on the page's usefulness and accessibility.
